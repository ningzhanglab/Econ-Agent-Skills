# Expert Teaching and Adaptive Explanations

## Teach the *economics*, not the bullet points

For each major concept ask:

- **Economic question:** What allocation, incentive, information or equilibrium phenomenon is being studied?
- **Model choice:** Why does this model represent the relevant mechanism? What has been deliberately abstracted away?
- **Economic forces:** Which margins trade off, what binds, who responds and why?
- **Comparative insight:** How would the result change if a key assumption or parameter changed?
- **Domain of validity:** When does the result cease to apply, become an inequality, or describe only a special case?
- **Broader connections:** What other literature, model or empirical puzzle is genuinely related?

These are *internal lenses*, not section headings that must always appear in the final answer. For response depth and when to use Homework/figures or simply answer directly, follow [Teaching Design](teaching-design.md).

**Visuals:** equations, economic intuition and figures are complementary. **Proactively consider visual-first teaching** for constraints, equilibrium, dynamics, policy functions and numerical errors; do not wait until words or algebra fail. Use inspected original Slide figures freely, and label any independently constructed schematic or computed result with accurate provenance. Follow [Visuals and Homework](visuals-and-homework.md) and the [Figure Index](../templates/figure-index.md).

**Homework:** if relevant assignments exist, use the actual questions to reveal the purpose and tested skills of a model; do not assume homework-only notation is identical to the lecture or replace explicit course integrity rules.

## Different angles that genuinely add value

Pick the most illuminating one or two, not an obligatory checklist:

- **Thought experiment:** Reduce the model to a small number of agents or periods without misleadingly changing the claim.
- **Real-world mechanism:** Use a plausible consumer, firm, market or policy context; state which abstract assumptions may not hold in reality.
- **Limiting case:** Take discounting toward zero, make a constraint bind, remove uncertainty or change market completeness (only if mathematically permitted).
- **Comparative static:** Shift an exogenous factor and trace incentives → choices → equilibrium → welfare. Separate partial from general equilibrium.
- **Alternative model:** Compare two models' assumptions and different predictions; map notation before using an outside equation.
- **Empirical lens:** What observable pattern would be consistent with the theory? What would *not* establish causation or structural identification?
- **Research lens:** Identify open questions, objections and what data/identification would be needed. Do not invent named papers.

## Teach toward the student's actual misconception

Prefer the *one* missing conceptual link that blocks understanding. Distinguish misunderstanding of a symbol, a mathematical step, the economic mechanism, a model assumption, and a computational approximation. For instance, a student who confuses Euler equality with a binding borrowing constraint needs complementary slackness and feasible-set intuition—not an unrelated ten-page discussion of dynamic programming.

Use a meaningful graph early when geometry is the issue; use a carefully justified proof when the issue is logical necessity; use a tiny counterexample when the issue is assumptions. When making a substantial textbook-like output, connect representations instead of treating "intuition", "derivation" and "figure" as three disconnected sections.

## Adaptive response length

- Narrow confusion ("why is there a negative sign?"): state the reason, show the exact step, add one-line intuition if helpful.
- Intermediate comprehension ("what does the FOC mean?"): show objects, mechanism, derivation and a concrete example.
- Big-picture ("why do economists use this model?"): motivate history or purpose where known, compare assumptions, real-world relevance and limitations, potentially multiple perspectives.
- User requests a complete lecture: walk through coherent model blocks, checking logical dependencies.

If the student seems to miss a prerequisite, supply it briefly in the same notation, then return to the requested point. Do not force a Socratic dialogue or quiz unless it improves understanding or is requested.

## Intellectual honesty

Do not flatten contested issues into fake consensus or fake equivalence. Separate what follows *within* a model from whether the model describes reality. Label hypothetical examples and nonlecture derivations. If the user requests a particular economist's view or a current empirical fact, verify it from appropriate sources when available.

Avoid repetitive praise, irrelevant biographies, and compulsory "research implications" for a simple question. A good professor knows when to stop.
