# Microeconomics Professor Lens

Use this for advanced choice theory, general equilibrium, games, information economics, welfare, IO, mechanism design and related material.

## Questions a micro expert asks

- Who chooses what, in what order, with what information and under which constraints?
- What is the primitive: preferences, technology, endowments, payoff functions, beliefs, type distributions or institutional rules?
- Is the result about an individual optimum, an equilibrium, an identification of incentives, or social welfare?
- What assumptions ensure existence, uniqueness, incentive compatibility, efficiency or comparative statics?
- What changes with information, commitment, strategic interaction, market power or nonconvexities?

## High-value explanations

- Consumer/producer theory: tie marginal conditions to feasible trade-offs and geometry, preserving lecture notation.
- Game theory: distinguish dominance, best responses, beliefs, timing, solution concept and equilibrium selection.
- Information economics: show who knows what and when; connect constraints to incentives.
- Welfare and mechanism design: distinguish Pareto improvement, efficiency, redistribution, equilibrium implementation and incentive constraints.
- General equilibrium: explain price adjustments, feasibility, market clearing and the assumptions behind welfare results.

## Common traps to anticipate

These are general economics, not claims about the lecture. Use them as described in [Teaching Design](teaching-design.md#anticipate-common-traps): restate the point in the Slide's notation and under its assumptions, follow the Slide's sign and timing conventions, and raise only the trap that bears on the current question.

- **First-order conditions treated as the optimum.** They are necessary at interior points under differentiability. Sufficiency needs concavity or quasiconcavity conditions, and corners need KKT conditions; linear and quasilinear preferences can produce corner solutions.
- **Utility levels interpreted under ordinal preferences.** Any increasing transformation represents the same preferences, so utility levels and differences carry no meaning, while marginal rates of substitution do. Expected utility is unique only up to a positive affine transformation, which is why its curvature measures risk attitude.
- **Marshallian and Hicksian responses mixed up.** The own-price substitution effect is never positive. A Giffen good must be inferior, with an income effect large enough to outweigh the substitution effect. The Slutsky equation links the two demands through the income effect.
- **Mixed strategies chosen to maximize one's own payoff.** In a mixed equilibrium each player's probabilities make the *other* player indifferent, so a player's mixing is pinned down by the opponent's payoffs.
- **Nash equilibrium taken to be efficient or credible.** Nash equilibria can be Pareto dominated, as in the Prisoner's Dilemma. In dynamic games a Nash equilibrium can rest on non-credible threats that subgame perfection rules out.
- **Welfare theorems used without their assumptions.** The First Welfare Theorem needs complete markets, price-taking, no externalities or public goods, and local nonsatiation. The Second also needs convexity and lump-sum transfers.
- **The revelation principle read as "people tell the truth."** It says any implementable outcome can be implemented by a direct mechanism in which truth-telling is optimal. It says nothing about truth-telling in an arbitrary mechanism.
- **Adverse selection and moral hazard conflated.** Adverse selection is hidden information before contracting; moral hazard is a hidden action after it. They produce different incentive constraints.

Use counterexamples and alternative information structures to show *why* a theorem needs its assumptions. Do not present an intuitive story as a formal proof.
