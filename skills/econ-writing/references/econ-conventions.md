# Economics Writing Conventions

Use these as defaults. A department's thesis rules, a journal's style guide, a course's homework instructions or an adviser's preference overrides them.

## General style

- Lead with the point. A paragraph opens with its claim; the evidence follows.
- Report magnitudes, not only significance. Say what an estimate means in units the reader cares about ("a one-standard-deviation increase in X raises Y by 0.2 percent of annual income"), then give its precision. Statistical significance and economic importance are separate statements.
- Match causal language to the identification. Words such as "effect", "causes", "raises" and "leads to" need a design that supports them; otherwise use the writer's descriptive terms ("is associated with", "predicts"). Do not upgrade or downgrade the writer's wording without flagging it.
- State assumptions where they are used, and name them the same way each time.
- Define every symbol and acronym at first use. Keep each section's notation consistent with the rest of the document.
- Use "we" in multi-author papers and in many single-author papers; keep whichever the writer uses. Use the present tense for what the paper does and shows, and the past tense for what was done to the data or what earlier papers found, unless the writer follows another convention consistently.
- Tables and figures should be readable on their own: a descriptive title, units, sample, the dependent variable, what the standard errors are and how they are clustered, and the meaning of any stars.

## Research papers

- **Title:** says what the paper studies, not a slogan.
- **Abstract** (often 100–150 words; follow the journal limit): question, approach or data, main result with a magnitude, and the main implication. No citations and no undefined acronyms.
- **Introduction:** the question and why it matters; what the paper does; the main results with numbers; how identification or the model works, in a few sentences; the contribution relative to the closest papers, stated specifically; and, if useful, a short roadmap. The contribution should be clear by the end of the first page or two.
- **Literature:** organized by idea, with the paper's position in each strand stated. A list of citations without that link does not do the job. Never add a reference the writer did not supply.
- **Model or theory:** environment and primitives, then agents' problems, equilibrium definition, and results. Explain what each assumption does for the result and what it rules out.
- **Data:** sources, sample construction, key variable definitions and summary statistics, with anything unusual about the sample stated plainly.
- **Empirical strategy:** the estimating equation, the parameter of interest, the identifying assumption in words, the main threats, and how the design addresses them. The estimand is named before the estimator.
- **Results:** main estimates with magnitudes and their economic meaning, then heterogeneity and mechanisms. Robustness checks are grouped by the threat they address.
- **Conclusion:** the main findings in a few sentences, the limitations that matter for interpretation, and specific open questions. No new results and no generic statement that more research is needed.

## Thesis chapters

Each chapter usually needs to stand alone as a paper, with its own introduction and contribution. A thesis introduction or overview chapter states the common question across chapters and what each chapter adds. Follow the university's formatting rules for front matter, citations and length.

## Referee reports

1. **Summary:** what the paper does and finds, in the authors' own terms, in one paragraph. This shows the editor that the referee understood the paper.
2. **Assessment:** a short paragraph on the contribution and whether the main conclusions follow from the evidence.
3. **Main concerns,** ranked, each with the problem, why it matters for the conclusions, and what analysis or change would address it.
4. **Minor comments:** exposition, presentation, typos.

The tone is direct and professional. Requests should be things the authors can do. Recommendations to the editor usually go in a separate confidential letter, not in the report.

## Research and policy reports

Open with a summary of the findings and what they imply for the reader's decision. Then give the question, data and method at the level of detail the audience needs, the results, the uncertainty around them, and the limitations. Technical material goes to an appendix.

## Homework write-ups

- Keep the course's notation and the method the writer chose; do not switch to a different approach.
- Each step says what was done and why it is allowed: which condition was imposed, which equation was substituted, which assumption was used.
- State the final answer clearly and with any units or conditions it depends on.
- Do not add steps, results or interpretation the writer has not produced. Point out gaps instead.
- Follow the course's rules on AI assistance and collaboration.
