# Expert Teaching: Angles of Explanation

[Teaching Design](teaching-design.md) decides *what* to teach: the mode, the likely bottleneck, why the model is built this way, and how long the answer should be. This file offers the *angles* an expert uses to make that explanation illuminating. Figures and Homework follow [Visuals and Homework](visuals-and-homework.md): proactively consider a figure, and use the actual assignment when one is relevant.

## Teach the *economics*, not the bullet points

For each major concept ask:

- **Economic question:** What allocation, incentive, information or equilibrium phenomenon is being studied?
- **Model choice:** Why does this model represent the relevant mechanism? What has been deliberately abstracted away?
- **Economic forces:** Which margins trade off, what binds, who responds and why?
- **Comparative insight:** How would the result change if a key assumption or parameter changed?
- **Domain of validity:** When does the result cease to apply, become an inequality, or describe only a special case?
- **Broader connections:** What other literature, model or empirical puzzle is genuinely related?

These are *internal lenses*, not section headings that must always appear in the final answer.

## Different angles that genuinely add value

Pick the most illuminating one or two, not an obligatory checklist:

- **Thought experiment:** Reduce the model to a small number of agents or periods without misleadingly changing the claim.
- **Real-world mechanism:** Use a plausible consumer, firm, market or policy context; state which abstract assumptions may not hold in reality.
- **Limiting case:** Take discounting toward zero, make a constraint bind, remove uncertainty or change market completeness (only if mathematically permitted).
- **Comparative static:** Shift an exogenous factor and trace incentives → choices → equilibrium → welfare. Separate partial from general equilibrium.
- **Alternative model:** Compare two models' assumptions and different predictions; map notation before using an outside equation.
- **Empirical lens:** What observable pattern would be consistent with the theory? What would *not* establish causation or structural identification?
- **Research lens:** Identify open questions, objections and what data/identification would be needed. Do not invent named papers.

A useful counterexample must actually satisfy the altered assumptions and contradict the proposed conclusion; an anecdote is not a theorem counterexample. See [Mathematical Reasoning](mathematical-reasoning.md).

## Intellectual honesty

Do not flatten contested issues into fake consensus or fake equivalence. Separate what follows *within* a model from whether the model describes reality. Label hypothetical examples and nonlecture derivations. If the user requests a particular economist's view or a current empirical fact, verify it from appropriate sources when available.

Avoid repetitive praise, irrelevant biographies, and compulsory "research implications" for a simple question. A good professor knows when to stop.
