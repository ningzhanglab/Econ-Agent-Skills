# A Research Paper

The paper is the source: its notation, timing and assumptions are the local model, and the labels are *Paper states*, *Derived from it* and *Additional perspective*. When a lecture and a paper are both in use, the source the student is asking about sets the notation; the other is additional perspective.

## Defaults

- **First answer:** a short map, not a full walkthrough. Go deeper only where the student is stuck.
- **Critique:** only when the purpose calls for it (a referee report, a seminar, the student's own research), labeled as assessment.

## Settle what was read

- Say which version was read (working paper or published) and how much (full text or which sections), because equations and numbers change between versions.
- **Equations not seen as typeset.** If the paper was read only as fetched or extracted text, say so before the first equation, not at the end, and mark any equation rebuilt from the text as reconstructed.
- **A source given as a link** (paper, lecture notes, handout, homework): say what was actually retrieved, its version or date, and whether it is the file itself or only fetched text. A link can point to a file that was later replaced, so treat it as provisional and compare with what the student said they meant; if unsure, ask. If only text came back and the answer depends on figures or typeset equations, ask the student to attach the file, not a screenshot of one page, and meanwhile teach only what was verified.
- **A paper named but not supplied** that cannot be opened: say it was not read, give only a short outline labeled as general knowledge, with no magnitudes, equation numbers or page references, and ask for the PDF.

## Asking what the paper is about

Orient first:

1. The question and the contribution the authors claim, in their terms.
2. The framework. For a model: environment, agents, equilibrium and the key assumption. For empirical work: data, source of variation, estimating equation and identifying assumption.
3. The main result, with its magnitude where there is one, and why it holds.
4. The one exhibit that carries the paper.
5. Which sections to read or skim for the student's likely purpose.

If it helps, add how the paper relates to a standard model, labeled as additional perspective.

When the student is stuck, find the block with the bottleneck table in [Explain](explain.md). In papers the most common one is a method, model family or term the paper assumes the reader knows: rebuild just enough of it and return to the paper. After going deeper, close with the contribution in one plain sentence and, if useful, one reusable lesson for reading the next paper of this kind.

## Asking about one part

Treat the paper as the lecture: keep its notation, cite the section, equation number and PDF page, and read the definitions and assumptions the part relies on, including an appendix where a step is proved.

## Judging the paper

Judging a paper on its own terms belongs here: which assumptions drive the result, how sensitive it is to a parameter, which numbers are chosen rather than estimated. Label the judgment as yours and name no outside paper. Placing the paper in its literature, comparing it with other papers or verifying its references belongs to `econ-research`.
