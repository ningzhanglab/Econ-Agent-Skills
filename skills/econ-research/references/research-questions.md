# Research Questions

A good economics research question names an economic object, a setting and a way to learn about it, and its answer would change what someone believes or does. This reference helps move from an interest to that kind of question. Some of the generating frameworks below are adapted from the CC0 [research-ideation](https://github.com/meleantonio/awesome-econ-ai-stuff) skill.

## Start from what the user has

Ask only what is missing, one or two questions at a time:

- **Starting point:** a phenomenon, a puzzle, a policy, a dataset, a method, or a paper they want to extend.
- **Field and approach:** applied micro, macro, theory, structural, econometric methods, or open.
- **Constraints:** data they can access, time (a second-year paper, a job-market paper, a thesis chapter), skills, and whether an adviser has set a direction.

## Ways to generate candidate questions

- **Puzzle:** a pattern that standard models do not predict, or two facts that seem inconsistent. Ask what would have to be true for both to hold.
- **Policy or event:** a policy change, reform, shock or rule with a discontinuity or staggered timing. Ask what it changed, for whom, and what it reveals about behavior.
- **Data:** a new or newly linked dataset. Ask which question it can answer that existing data could not; a dataset alone is not a question.
- **Mechanism:** a known result whose channel is unclear. Ask which mechanisms are consistent with it and what evidence would separate them.
- **Extension:** a strong paper. Ask what changes in another setting, population or period, under a relaxed assumption, or with an outcome they could not measure.
- **Method transfer:** a tool from one field applied to another field's question, where the tool fixes a known problem.
- **Measurement:** an object everyone uses but nobody measures well. A better measure can be the contribution.

For each candidate, state it as one sentence with a clear object: "How does X affect Y for population Z?", "Why does pattern P occur?", "What is the value of parameter θ in setting S?".

## Test each candidate

Run these checks and report them plainly, in this order:

1. **Is it a question?** It has an answer that could come out more than one way. "Study inequality" is a topic; "Did the 2015 minimum-wage increase in state S compress the wage distribution below the median?" is a question.
2. **Is it already answered?** Search the literature before judging (see [Literature Search](literature-search.md)). Name the closest papers found and what they leave open. If the search was quick, say the judgment is provisional.
3. **What is the contribution?** One sentence: what will we know after the project that we do not know now? Typical forms: new evidence on an open question, a cleaner design for a contested one, a new mechanism, a new setting where theory predicts something different, a new measure, a model that reconciles conflicting facts. "First to use dataset D" is not a contribution unless D answers something new.
4. **Can it be answered?** Name the variation or model that would identify the answer and the main threat to it. For empirical questions: what is the source of variation, and why is it plausibly unrelated to other determinants of the outcome? For theory: what is the key assumption, and is the result likely to be more than a restatement of it? Flag it when no credible design is apparent.
5. **Is it feasible for this user?** Data access, time, cost and skills. Flag restricted data, long application processes or field work.
6. **Who cares?** Which literature, policy or decision would the answer move?

Present several candidates side by side when that helps, but give a recommendation and say why.

## Common weak questions

- Too broad: a whole field in one question ("What causes growth?").
- No variation: the treatment does not vary, or varies only with things that also drive the outcome.
- No data: the outcome or treatment cannot be observed.
- Already settled: a large, consistent literature answers it, and the project adds only another setting without a reason that setting matters.
- Data in search of a question: the project is defined by a dataset rather than by what it would teach us.
- Answer known in advance: the result follows mechanically from an assumption.

## Output

Lead with the recommended question in one sentence. Then give, briefly: the contribution, the closest verified papers and what they leave open, the likely identification or modeling approach and its main threat, feasibility notes, and the next concrete step (a paper to read, a dataset to check, a fact to establish). Keep the user's own wording for their idea and key terms.
